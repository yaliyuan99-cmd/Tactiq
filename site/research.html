The research behind Tactiq

This project asks one question at two levels. The conceptual question, answered by the design study: why does the affordability–reliability trade-off in eyes-free phone access persist, and what interaction model could resolve it? The empirical question, answered by the physics study: can the cheapest plausible sensing channel tell Tactiq's eight contact points apart? Its pass/fail criteria were written down in advance.

Study one: the design study

Beyond Screen Readers: design principles for affordable, braille-independent, eyes-free smartphone command layers for blind users.

The study starts from verified field data. 91.3% of screen-reader users use one on mobile, and only 38% use braille output at all. Out-of-the-box voice assistants have been measured around 50–60% accuracy on atypical speech, and unadapted state-of-the-art recognition still misses most conversational disordered speech. Reliable tactile hardware runs $799 to $15,500. Output is solved and free; input is slow, exposed, or expensive.

Why exactly nine commands?

Because command capacity is limited by discrimination, not ambition. Apple's AssistiveTouch ships four hand gestures; Meta's sEMG wristband decodes about nine on unseen users; the closest published ring (EFRing) classified nine microgestures at 89.5% accuracy. Tactiq's own capacity analysis finds a knee at eight contact points: doubling to sixteen would add roughly +0.31 bits of theoretical capacity while worst-command accuracy collapses from 87% to about 50%. Nine commands on eight anchored points is not a limitation: it is the honest answer to a measured question.

The study derives nine binding design principles. Among them:

  • Augment the screen reader, never replace it. The ring carries the high-frequency commands; VoiceOver and TalkBack keep doing everything else.
  • One hand is enough. The other hand may be holding a cane, a harness, or a coffee.
  • Feedback must be silent-capable. Haptics first; audio optional, one ear only.
  • Nothing fires without a deliberate wake. A squeeze opens a short command window; the target is at most one false activation per hour.
  • Critical actions are separated by duration, not location. The emergency pathway arms only on a sustained five-second hold.
  • Affordability is a design requirement, not a pricing decision. Consumer parts, consumer manufacturing, consumer price.

Study two: the physics study

How accurately can the two-dimensional position of a small magnet be determined by trilateration of distances inferred from magnetic field strength at three fixed points?

The cheapest plausible sensing channel for the tap grid is a passive magnet worn on the thumb, located by low-cost magnetometers using the inverse-cube dipole law: no camera, no battery on the thumb, no per-user machine learning. The study builds a fully physical simulation of that channel: real sensor noise (QMC5883L, 0.2 µT), Sydney's geomagnetic background, magnet tilt, gain error, and an estimator proven to reach the theoretical accuracy bound.

≈ 2 mm against 20–25 mm targets

Headline simulated results, under stated conditions: 1.65 mm localization error at the array centre (≈2.0 mm with the realistic error budget; ≈4.4 mm off-centre), with 97% of the workspace under 10 mm. Adjacent tap points on an adult hand sit 20–25 mm apart. The conditions matter and are reported with the result: magnet tilt under roughly 12 degrees, per-sensor gain cross-calibration (an uncalibrated ±5% gain error alone costs ≈3.3 mm), and a minimum sensor standoff to avoid saturation.

The hand-scale model also says where the real risk lives: with realistic sensor noise the eight points are separated by 50–88 standard deviations — sensor noise is not the bottleneck; thumb aim is. If real tap scatter is around 4 mm, per-contact accuracy models at 86–99%; at 6 mm, the worst contact drops toward 79%. Which is exactly why the bench experiment measures real thumbs before anything else.

What would change our minds

  • If measured thumb scatter is ≈6 mm and no design response recovers 95%, the eight-point grid shrinks or the grid is redesigned.
  • If blind users, interviewed before ever seeing the concept, don't rank eyes-free input among their top daily frictions, the premise — not the product — gets rethought.
  • If the wake squeeze costs more time than serial navigation saves, the gate is redesigned before anything ships.
  • If worn accuracy stays under 90% after calibration, this sensing channel is abandoned for the next candidate.

Status (July 2026): both studies complete in simulation; bench experiment runs August–October 2026; AUSSEF submission 11 November 2026; ISEF 2027 pathway. Interviews and prototype trials with blind users are planned through community organisations — ethics approval first, accessible consent, and paid participation.

Nine commands on the fingers you can always feel.

Tactiq is a smart-ring concept for silent, eyes-free phone control, built for blind and low-vision users. The thumb taps points on your own fingers: no screen, no voice, one hand. It works with VoiceOver and TalkBack, not instead of them.

Read the research How it works

What it is

One ring, worn on the index finger of either hand. The thumb taps the fingers of the same hand, eight contact points you can always feel, and each tap becomes a phone command, without a screen, without a voice, without reaching for anything.

The interface lives on the body. The hand is the controller.

Setup is voice-guided from first pairing. Daily use needs no sight, no speech, and no screen. The ring is a slim, matte consumer object — no clinical aesthetic, no accessibility iconography, nothing that announces why you wear it.

How it works

The thumb is the pointer; the fingers are the buttons. The tips and bases of the four fingers form eight contact points carrying nine commands. The ring is idle until you wake it with a deliberate squeeze of the ring body, which opens a short command window, so it rides through handshakes, typing, and carried bags without misfiring. The design target is at most one false activation per hour.

Every command is confirmed by touch: a small set of distinct haptic patterns closes the loop within the same interaction, so mistakes are caught before they have consequences. For denser information, an optional single earbud speaks. One ear only, because ambient sound is how blind users read the street.

Two things are deliberately absent. There is no text entry in the fixed command set, and there is no "clear field" gesture anywhere on the device. Destructive actions have no place one tap away from daily commands.

The problem it solves

For blind users the smartphone is not a convenience. It is infrastructure for independence. Screen readers solved output years ago, for free: VoiceOver and TalkBack speak the screen aloud on every phone, and 91% of screen-reader users now run one on mobile. The stubborn, unsolved half is input.

Every routine action pays a navigation tax

Issuing a command still means serially stepping focus through a screen you cannot see — for the same handful of actions, dozens of times a day.

Voice is conditionally reliable at best

It fails in noise: on the bus, in the street, in shared rooms. It has been measured at roughly 50–60% accuracy on atypical speech. And even when it works, it broadcasts your business to everyone in earshot. A perfectly accurate assistant still isn't private.

The reliable alternative is priced like medical equipment

Refreshable braille displays run from about $800 to $15,500, and they assume braille, which most people who lose sight in adulthood never learn. Only 38% of screen-reader users use braille output at all.

Reliable, affordable, eyes-free has been a pick-two.
Tactiq is built on the premise that it doesn't have to be.

Smart-ring hardware is now a mature consumer category. Tactiq's bench prototype targets under $60 in components, and the product targets the price of a pair of wireless earbuds, not the price of a laptop. Sources for every figure above are on the research page.

The research behind it

Tactiq is the product of two linked studies, and it makes no claim the research doesn't back.

The design study

Nine commands, on purpose

Why does the affordability–reliability trade-off persist, and what could resolve it? The study derives nine binding design principles and answers a measured question: how many commands can a wearable reliably distinguish? Published systems from the world's biggest labs land at four to nine. Tactiq's grid is designed at that frontier, anchored to points the hand can feel.

About the design study

The physics study

~2 mm vs 20–25 mm

Can the cheapest plausible sensing channel tell eight contact points apart? Simulating a passive magnet located by three low-cost magnetometers and the inverse-cube law yields roughly 2 mm of localization error against contact points spaced 20–25 mm apart — an order-of-magnitude margin, in simulation, under stated conditions.

Our commitment to honest engineering

Every performance figure on this page is a target with a pre-registered test — not an achieved result.

That is deliberate. The pass/fail criteria were written down before the experiments, and they are public on the research page. It is what separates engineering from marketing.

Who it's for

First — blind and low-vision users

Tactiq removes the daily tax of driving a screen you cannot see. Setup is voice-guided from first pairing; daily use needs no sight, no speech, and no screen. The hand always knows where it is.

Second — people for whom touchscreens are hard

Older adults and users with fine-motor difficulty trade a glass surface's precision demands for the familiarity of tapping your own fingers.

Also — anyone whose eyes or voice are spoken for

Commuters, cyclists, people in meetings, hands-busy workers. Tactiq never asks its users to identify as disabled. It is a control surface that happens to be life-changing for the people who need it most.
